What belongs on a Colorado mover’s estimate and contract

Before anyone lifts a box, you should be holding paper. Colorado statute lists what that paper must contain. Use this as a checklist.

The written estimate

The Colorado PUC’s consumer guidance treats both a written estimate and a signed contract as required of permitted household goods movers, and recommends asking for an in-person (or video) look at your belongings so the estimate reflects reality. Phone-only numbers are easy to inflate or shrink later. Source: Colorado PUC, movers.

The contract, line by line

C.R.S. 40-10.1-505 says the mover must give you a contract, signed and dated by both of you, at or before the moment work begins. It must carry:

  • The mover’s name, phone number and a physical address where staff can be reached in business hours.
  • The date the document was prepared and the planned moving date.
  • Your name, address and phone, and the pickup and delivery addresses.
  • Where your goods will be held if they are stored or kept during a payment dispute: name, phone and street address.
  • Itemized rates and services for transportation, extras and storage.
  • At least two accepted forms of payment out of: cash; cashier’s check, money order or traveler’s check; a personal check; a credit card, with a clear statement of which apply.
  • The sentence that the company is permitted with the Colorado PUC as a mover, plus its permit number.

The mover also has to hand you a consumer advisement and keep a record of it for at least three years. Per the PUC’s summary, the advisement tells you the total can change and that Colorado offers an arbitration process for disputes. Source: C.R.S. 40-10.1-505.

Where “binding” and “non-binding” come in

Those two words are federal terms for interstate moves (49 CFR Part 375): a mover may charge for a binding estimate but not a non-binding one, and cannot change an estimate once loading has begun. If the company calls your in-town job “binding,” ask them to write down exactly what is and is not included. The paperwork, not the label, is what protects you.

Before you sign

  1. Read the whole thing, including the small print about liability limits.
  2. Strike or question anything blank.
  3. Keep a copy and take phone photos of every page.
